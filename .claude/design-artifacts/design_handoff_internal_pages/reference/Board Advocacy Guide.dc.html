District 65 School Board Advocacy Guide

Speak up. Show up. Help D65 Thrive.

Why Engage the School Board?

School boards make decisions that directly affect every student, teacher, and family in our community — everything from curriculum and budgets to safety policies and school hours. Board members are elected officials who are accountable to you. Your voice matters more than you may think.

Community members who show up consistently and speak clearly can shift board priorities, change votes, and build lasting relationships with decision-makers.

Know Our School Board

How Boards Work

Our board has 7 elected members who set policy, approve budgets, and hire the superintendent. They typically meet around twice a month in public sessions. One meeting is the Committee of the Whole, where all members discuss topics in depth without taking final action; the other is the formal Board Meeting where more formal actions take place. Both have time allotted for public comments at the beginning of the meeting.

Speaking at Public Comment

Public comment is your right to address elected officials directly. Most meetings allow 3 minutes per speaker; if there are a lot of speakers, they'll pro-rate to keep comments to an hour (as short as ~90 seconds). Here's how to make every second count.

Before You Go

  1. Review the agenda — you can comment on agenda items or general matters.
  2. Prepare your remarks in writing. 3 minutes is roughly 330–450 words; practice out loud.
  3. Know that board members will listen but won't comment or engage in dialogue during your remarks.

Structure Your Statement

0:00–0:20Introduce yourself: name, your connection to the district (parent, teacher, resident, student)
0:20–1:00State your issue clearly — one focused topic is more powerful than many
1:00–1:45Provide evidence: data, personal story, or community impact
1:45–2:30Make a specific ask — what do you want the board to do?
2:30–3:00Thank the board

The Meeting

  1. Arrive 15–20 minutes early — seating and sign-up lines can fill up.
  2. Sign up on the sheet on a table right outside the meeting room, to the left as you walk in.
  3. After signing, go in and take an open seat.
  4. After a few minutes of business, the board secretary reads off groups of names. When yours is called, line up behind the podium.
  5. When it's your turn, the secretary starts the timer. If you finish early, return to your seat; if the timer goes off, wrap up the last sentence or two.
  6. Many people stay to hear the rest of the meeting — it's also fine to leave once you're done.

Afterward, send a copy of your comments to schoolboard@district65.net.

Dos & don'ts

Do: speak calmly and respectfully, use "I" statements, stick to your time limit, leave contact info.
Don't: attack individuals personally, go over time, bring props, or bring more issues than you can cover in your allotted minutes.

Written Advocacy

You don't have to speak in person to make an impact. Written communications, especially submitted before a vote, are read and logged into the official record. Send comments to schoolboard@district65.net, which reaches all board members and the board secretary.

Email Best Practices

  • Subject line: be specific — "Support for Expanded Mental Health Counselors — Board Meeting June 10"
  • Open with your connection to the district and the specific issue
  • Keep it around 300 words — busy board members may skim long emails
  • End with a clear ask and your contact information
  • CC the superintendent or other relevant cabinet members for added visibility
  • Follow up if you don't hear back within two weeks

Letters to the Board

Formal written letters submitted before a board meeting are entered into official minutes. A brief, factual letter from 10–20 or more community members on the same issue carries significant weight.

Power move

Organize a letter-writing campaign. Coordinate a group to send individual emails or letters about the same issue in the week before a key vote — volume signals community concern.

Building Long-Term Influence

One-time appearances matter, but sustained engagement is what creates lasting change. The most effective advocates treat the school board like a long-term relationship, not a single transaction.

Show Up Consistently

  • Attend as many meetings as is feasible for you
  • Show up even when your issue isn't on the agenda — presence signals engagement
  • Introduce yourself to board members before or after meetings
  • We recognize meeting time and location make it hard for many to attend — in-person speaking is powerful, but there are many other ways to make a difference

Build Community

  • Connect with parent-teacher organizations, neighborhood associations, and civic groups
  • Partner with others — a coalition of voices is harder to ignore than one
  • Share meeting dates, agendas, and your comments widely in community networks and social media
  • Organize carpools or childcare so more community members can attend

Work the System

  • Request meetings with individual board members between public sessions — they may respond
  • Submit public records requests (FOIA) for budgets, contracts, or reports that inform your advocacy
  • Participate in board-sponsored community surveys and input sessions
  • Apply to serve on board advisory committees when opportunities arise — it puts you in the room earlier

Sample Public Comment Script

Adapt this template to your specific issue. Fill in the bracketed sections with your own details.

Sample script

"Good evening. My name is [Your Name], and I am a [parent/resident/teacher] in D65. I'm here tonight to speak about [specific issue]. This matters to me because [brief personal connection or community impact]. [Share one data point, story, or example that illustrates the problem or opportunity.] I'm asking the board to [specific, actionable request] by [realistic timeframe]. Thank you for your service to our schools."

Advocacy Checklist

  • I know my school board's meeting schedule and how to sign up for public comment
  • I have contact information for my board (schoolboard@district65.net)
  • I can state my issue and specific ask in 3 minutes or less
  • I have identified at least 2–3 others who share my concern
  • I have reviewed recent board minutes or agendas related to my issue
  • I know the next meeting date and have it on my calendar

Democracy works when communities show up. Every meeting you attend, every comment you make, and every neighbor you bring along strengthens the schools our community deserves.
