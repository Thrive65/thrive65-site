District 65 serves {s-now} K-8 students this fall, {s-drop} fewer than at its 2016-17 peak. Most of the drop comes from smaller kindergarten classes, and the district's 2022 enrollment study predicted this. The latest birth records point to more of the same.

## 1. `ch-drop`: A steady decline since 2016

Enrollment peaked at {s-peak} in 2016-17 and has fallen every year since, to {s-now} this fall. That is {s-pct} fewer students.

The pandemic years were the steepest, but the decline started before 2020 and has continued after.

## 2. `ch-nation`: Steeper than the nation and Chicago

From fall 2019 to fall 2024, public school K-8 enrollment fell {s-us} nationally and {s-cps} in Chicago. District 65 fell {s-d65}.

Most of the difference is in kindergarten. Nationally, kindergarten enrollment partly recovered after 2020, while District 65's kept shrinking. That points to something happening locally.

Source: National Center for Education Statistics, Digest 2025, table 203.10; CPS FY2027 Budget; Illinois Report Card.

## 3. `ch-peers` (tall step): Most nearby districts are shrinking too, though less

Among {nb-n} nearby suburban elementary districts, the typical one changed {nb-med} from 2018-19 to 2025-26, and only {nb-grew} grew. District 65's drop of {nb-d65} is the second steepest, behind only Avoca 37, a district of about 500 students. Each of the 13 peer districts District 65 cites lost a smaller share or grew; the closest was Joliet 86 at −19.7%.

Across Cook County, of {ck-n} elementary districts with at least 1,500 students, District 65 has the {ck-rank} steepest decline, behind {ck-ahead}.

## 4. `ch-private`: Private schools gained some students during the pandemic, but don't explain our decline in enrollment

Private K-8 enrollment in the boundary rose by about 200 from 2019-20 to 2022-23, then fell back to about 940.

In fall 2020 and 2021, District 65 lost 444 students who were already enrolled, net, for any reason, including moving away. About two-thirds of them would be in high school or graduated by now, so even if their families came back, few of them would still be in District 65 for long.

Source: Illinois State Board of Education nonpublic school enrollment; District 65 counts. Note: Private school data is for schools within Evanston. A family choosing Baker School in Wilmette (148 students, not all Evanston) does not appear here, and some children included in totals at Evanston private schools live elsewhere and would not be D65.

## 5. `ch-eths`: More District 65 8th graders now stay for ETHS

Evanston Township High School (ETHS) reports where each class went to 8th grade. The share of District 65 8th graders who went on to ETHS rose from about 84% for the class of 2026 to about 93% for the class of 2029.

Private and parochial schools send 11% to 14% of each freshman class. Fewer freshmen now arrive from out of state or elsewhere in Illinois: 59 for the class of 2026, 25 for the class of 2029.

Source: District 202 feeder report, fall 2025; District 65 Opening of Schools reports.

## 6. `ch-classes`: Today's classes started smaller

Most classes in school this fall are close to the size they were in kindergarten. The oldest, who were in early elementary school during the pandemic, are somewhat smaller. Together they are only about {s-sincek} students smaller than when they started.

So nearly all of the drop since 2016-17 comes from smaller kindergarten classes. Families leaving explains only a small part.

Source: District enrollment by grade: state Illinois Report Card counts for fall 2020 to 2025, district counts for other years.

## 7. `ch-births`: Fewer children were born here

Births behind each kindergarten class fell from about 1,050 for the class of 2015 to about 720 for the class of 2025 in Kofron's data. County records, which count somewhat lower, show about 610 for this fall's class and about 580 for the class of 2028. The decline has slowed but not stopped. Births after 2023 are not yet published, so the shading shows a range: births holding at the 2023 level at the top, and falling at the pace of Kofron's trend line, about 32 fewer a year, at the bottom. The dotted line continues the recent pace of about 3% fewer a year. By the class of 2031, that puts births between about 480 and 580. The Thrive65 projections later on this page use the top of that range; at the recent pace, K-5 enrollment in 2031-32 would be about 85 students lower, and at Kofron's pace about 165 lower.

This fall {s-k} children started kindergarten, while {s-8} are in 8th grade. Each year the class leaving is larger than the class arriving.

The children who will start kindergarten through fall 2028 have already been born, so the size of those classes is largely set.

Source: Kofron study birth data; Cook County Department of Public Health birth reports for Evanston, scaled up 8% for Skokie.

## 8. `ch-forecast`: The district's own study saw this coming

The 2022 Kofron study, done for the district's facilities planning, forecast {s-kmid} students for this fall. The actual count was {s-now}.

It pointed to births falling steadily since 2010 and fewer young families moving in. The 2024 McKibben study expected more young families and is running a little high.

Split into grades K-5 and 6-8, Kofron's middle forecast has stayed within a few percent of actual enrollment in both. That comparison is at the foot of the page.

Source: Kofron, ES65 Geodemographic Study (2022); McKibben Demographic Consulting (2024); both scaled to district counts.

## 9. `ch-ahead`: What comes next

Both studies expect about 4,500 to 5,300 K-8 students by 2031-32. Thrive65's update of Kofron's method, starting from this fall's actual count, points to about {s-t65} ({s-t65r}). Using the district's capacity method, today's buildings hold about 8,150 students, and about 7,200 after the two proposed closures.

The Thrive65 update uses county birth records for each kindergarten class. It assumes births hold at the 2023 level, so if they keep falling, enrollment will land nearer the low end.

## 10. `ch-levers`: What the district can change, and how far that goes

Every family that chooses District 65 matters. It also helps to know how many students each possibility could bring. If every K-8 student at a private school inside the boundary enrolled in District 65, that would add about {s-priv}. That is close to what private schools here have enrolled for decades: in the years with complete records since the late 1990s, between about 850 and 1,200 K-8 students. Of the students who left in fall 2020 and 2021, about 150 are still young enough for grades K-8.

Housing that young families can afford and programs that draw families from nearby could add students too, though there is no good estimate yet of how many. Taken together, the options within reach are measured in hundreds of students. By 2031-32, today's buildings will have room for {s-gap} more students than the district expects to serve, and {s-gap2} more even after the two proposed closures.

Bringing families back is worth the effort, but it cannot fill that space on its own. Decisions about buildings should start from the expected number of students.

Source: This chapter is Thrive65's view. Private schools: Illinois State Board of Education nonpublic enrollment reports, 2010-11 to 2025-26, and the National Center for Education Statistics Private School Survey, 1997-98 to 2017-18 (Evanston schools, years when all the larger schools reported). Students who left: net change in grade counts, fall 2020 and 2021. Empty seats: the district's building capacities from its Nov. 6, 2026 utilization table (7,212 K-8 seats after the proposed closures; about 8,150 with Dewey and Lincolnwood, using the district's method for those two schools) minus the Thrive65 middle projection for 2031-32. The district's capacity count leaves out STEP and RISE special education classrooms and their students.
