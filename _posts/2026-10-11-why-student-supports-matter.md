# **What's at Stake When Student Supports Are Cut**

## **At a glance**

On October 13, District 65's administration will present about \$20 million in reductions, and administrators have said programs not mandated by law are under review. This sheet explains why the choice of *which* cuts matters so much for students' long-term prospects.

* **A legal mandate is a floor.** Laws require D65 to provide a core program and specific services, especially for students with disabilities. Many of the supports that make school success likely, such as early childhood, counselors and social workers, reading intervention, and busing, sit above that floor.  
* **Gaps in D65 form early and then hold steady.** Research presented in Evanston found that most of the gap is already in place by 3rd grade, and that students grow at similar rates afterward ([Evanston RoundTable, May 17, 2017](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2017/05/17/study-finds-achievement-opportunity-gaps-are-due-primarily-to-differences-that-occur-before-third-grade/); updated D65 data in the [Education Scorecard fact sheet](https://educationscorecard.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/report_IL_1714460_evanston-ccsd-65.pdf) and the [D65 Achievement and Accountability Report 2025-26](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1790889798/district65net/c7yzt05rxm3x7jcr3lod/ENGLISH-SY26AchievementandAccountabilityReport.pdf), p. 26). Supports in the early grades are a critical place where the district can change a child's trajectory.  
* **Spending cuts have measurable effects on students.** After the Great Recession, \$1,000 less per student led to lower test scores and fewer students starting college, with the largest harm in lower-income communities ([Jackson, Wigger, and Xiong, 2021, summarized in Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/test-scores-and-college-going-declined-after-great-recession-spending-cuts/); [full study](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3102021)).  
* **How a district spends matters as much as how much.** Illinois districts with similar poverty levels show very different results, which means careful, evidence-based choices can protect students even while budgets shrink.

The district has said the same things matter. In its September 14 presentation, the administration listed what it heard from the community, including supporting the most vulnerable students, keeping staff who support students' mental health and social-emotional needs, keeping class sizes small, and access to arts, athletics, and extracurriculars. It called these the lens for its decisions, while acknowledging there will be an impact ([D65 board presentation, Sept. 14, 2026](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/FileViewerOrPublic/1247?file=14421838), slide 26).

The ask: judge each proposed cut by its likely effect on students, and protect the supports with the strongest evidence and the fewest substitutes. A companion sheet covers [early childhood education](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/f14e45ec-33bd-41a7-807c-f8a23b12d445) in detail.

## **What "non-mandated" means**

A non-mandated program is one no state or federal law requires D65 to offer. That label describes legal obligation. It says nothing about how much a program matters to students or how much it saves.

The district has not released its list. Based on district presentations and community discussion, Thrive65's September community briefing grouped what may be at risk into two categories ([Thrive65 Community Briefing, Sept. 2026](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1nTz0e6fNV2vHeoG9F_-Z5edXbZ-3-E4o/view), slide 10; see also [Evanston RoundTable, Sept. 20](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2026/09/20/thrive65-new-district-65-parent-advocacy-group/), 2026).

| Non-mandated programs at risk | Other cuts/fees that have been raised |
| ----- | ----- |
| Early Head Start and Head Start | Special education spending |
| Preschool for All | Counselors and librarians |
| School Age Child Care and other child care programs | Instructional enhancements beyond the state-required curriculum |
| Optional components of magnet and choice programs | AVID, an academic support program for students who would be first in their families to attend college |
| Athletics, clubs, and other extracurricular activities | FACE (Family and Community Engagement) liaisons |
| Field trips and summer enrichment | New or higher fees for families,and a possible referendum |

These are possibilities raised in public discussion, not a district proposal. The October 13 package will show which are actually on the table.

**Where non-mandated programs fit in the district's plan.** The administration's September 14 presentation sets an FY28 target of about \$20 million, roughly 10% of a budget of about \$200 million, built from six areas: staffing and compensation, non-mandated programs, district footprint (closing at least two more schools), transportation, other purchased services, and new revenue ([D65 board presentation, Sept. 14, 2026](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/FileViewerOrPublic/1247?file=14421838), slides 27, 28, 30). The presentation does not say how much is expected from non-mandated programs. That share is one of the most important numbers to watch on October 13\.

**These cuts come on top of earlier ones.** The district reports about \$26 million in reductions and more than 170 full-time positions eliminated across FY25 to FY27. Those rounds already reduced transportation, summer programming, and School Age Child Care services ([D65 board presentation, Sept. 14, 2026](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/FileViewerOrPublic/1247?file=14421838), slide 15). When a program has been cut before, a further cut can be the one that ends it.

Many of these sit on both sides of the line. A few examples:

* **Social workers and paraprofessionals.** Services written into a student's IEP (Individualized Education Program), including social work services and classroom support, are legally required under federal special education law ([34 CFR 300.34](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-34/section-300.34); [34 CFR 300.320](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-34/section-300.320)). The same staff often also support students without IEPs. How many positions beyond IEP requirements a district funds is generally a local staffing decision, so ask the administration which proposed reductions are tied to IEP service minutes and which are not.  
* **Transportation.** Illinois requires districts to transport students who live 1.5 miles or more from school ([105 ILCS 5/29-3](https://www.ilga.gov/legislation/ilcs/fulltext.asp?DocName=010500050K29-3)), and transportation listed in an IEP is required. Busing for shorter distances, and for optional programs like preschool, is a district choice. D65's FY27 budget puts transportation spending at about \$10.3 million ([D65 FY27 Final Budget Memo](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/FileViewerOrPublic/1247?file=14472808)).  
* **Early childhood.** Preschool special education is required; Head Start and Preschool for All are optional and largely grant-funded. See the [early childhood sheet](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/f14e45ec-33bd-41a7-807c-f8a23b12d445).  
* **Special education Maintenance of Effort.** Federal law generally bars a district from reducing its local spending on special education below the prior year's level, with limited exceptions such as fewer students with disabilities or departing special education staff replaced by lower-paid qualified staff ([34 CFR 300.203](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-34/section-300.203); [34 CFR 300.204](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-34/section-300.204)). Any proposal to reduce it should explain which exception applies, since a district that falls short can be required to repay federal funds from local dollars.

When the October 13 package comes out, it will help to sort each item three ways: what is truly optional, what partly supports a legal requirement, and what saves less than it appears because of grants or shifted costs.

## **Gaps form early in D65 and then hold steady**

Stanford researcher Sean Reardon, who analyzed about 215 million test scores from nearly every U.S. school district, presented his findings at Evanston Township High School in May 2017\. His central point for Evanston: gaps by race and income were already wide by 3rd grade, and students in every group grew at roughly the same rate from 3rd to 8th grade, so the gaps changed little ([Evanston RoundTable, May 17, 2017](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2017/05/17/study-finds-achievement-opportunity-gaps-are-due-primarily-to-differences-that-occur-before-third-grade/)). He also found that D65's Black students outscored Black students in 78% of U.S. districts, and that the size of Evanston's gap reflects how far ahead white and affluent students start (same source).

Reardon's team has since updated these figures. The Education Scorecard, a joint project of Stanford's Educational Opportunity Project and Harvard's Center for Education Policy Research, reports D65 results through spring 2024 in grade levels above or below the 2019 national average for grades 3 to 8 ([Education Scorecard, D65 fact sheet, Feb. 2025](https://educationscorecard.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/02/report_IL_1714460_evanston-ccsd-65.pdf)):

| Student group | Math 2019 | Math 2024 | Reading 2019 | Reading 2024 |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| All D65 students | \+1.76 | \+1.09 | \+1.13 | \+0.92 |
| White | \+4.25 | \+3.08 | \+3.33 | \+2.74 |
| Asian | \+3.53 | \+2.77 | \+2.46 | \+1.95 |
| Hispanic | \+0.13 | \-0.40 | \-0.73 | \-0.63 |
| Black | \-1.10 | \-1.84 | \-1.46 | \-1.67 |
| Illinois, all students | \-0.03 | \-0.32 | \+0.11 | \-0.26 |

D65 as a whole still scores above both the state and national averages. The gap between white and Black students remains close to five grade levels in math and four and a half in reading. The gaps narrowed slightly since 2019 mainly because white students' scores fell more, while Black students lost ground in both subjects. The fact sheet also reports that chronic absenteeism in grades 3 to 8 rose 11 percentage points between 2019 and 2023\. These results describe outcomes by grade 3 and later; they reinforce that the starting gap is large, but they do not by themselves show what caused it. The scorecard does not report D65 results by family income.

D65's own data shows the same pattern from a different angle:

* **Kindergarten readiness fell from 56.2% to 39.4%** between fall 2017 and fall 2019, by the district's literacy measure ([Evanston RoundTable, Jan. 20, 2020](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2020/01/20/school-district-65-2019-achievement-report-big-drop-in-students-who-are-kindergarten-ready-no-gains-in-college-readiness/)).  
* **Starting points shaped the next four years.** D65's 2016 analysis found the K-3 reading gap narrowed among children who arrived kindergarten-ready and widened among those who did not ([D65 Report on Black Student Achievement, 2016](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1698044910/district65net/zukqpjplkyaqdixkobbu/2016_Black_Student_Achievement_Report.pdf), pp. 14-15).  
* **By the upper grades the gaps were large, and they still are.** On the 2019 state test, 63% of white students met or exceeded English standards, compared with 25% of Hispanic students, 18% of Black students, 18% of low-income students, and 11% of students with IEPs ([Evanston RoundTable, Nov. 30, 2019](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2019/11/30/state-test-shows-significantly-fewer-district-65-students-are-on-track-to-college-readiness-than-five-years-ago-declines-at-eighth-grade/)). Current results on the state's revised scale appear in the table below; the scales differ, so the 2019 and 2026 percentages should not be compared directly.  
* **Between 2019 and 2025, overall scores recovered in reading but the gaps did not close.** On the state test (original scale), the share of D65 students meeting English standards went from 43.8% in 2019 to 44.9% in 2023 ([Evanston RoundTable, Nov. 13, 2023](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2023/11/13/huge-percentages-of-district-65-students-are-not-ready-for-the-next-grade-level-in-ela-and-math-and-not-on-track-to-college-and-career-readiness/)) and 53.5% in 2024 ([Evanston RoundTable, Nov. 6, 2024](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2024/11/06/analysis-some-good-news-on-district-65-test-results-but-big-gaps-in-achievement-persist/)). The gap held: in English, 64% of white students and 19% of Black students met standards in 2023, and about 72% and 27% in 2024 (same sources). Math was flat overall, at 42.8% in 2024 and 42.1% in 2025 ([Evanston RoundTable, Sept. 18, 2025](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2025/09/18/evanston-skokie-district-65-achievement-report-2025/)); in 2023, 63% of white students and 12% of Black students met math standards ([Evanston RoundTable, Dec. 4, 2023](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2023/12/04/district-65-math-scores-rebound-but-opportunity-gap-persists-report-says/)). On the district's college-readiness measure in math, the share on track fell between 2017 and 2024 for every group: white students from 81% to 69.6%, Hispanic students from 35% to 28.5%, and Black students from 22% to 15.1% ([Evanston RoundTable, July 27, 2024](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2024/07/27/school-district-65-trends-on-college-readiness-decline-in-math-flat-in-reading/)).  
* **Recent results are mixed.** In 2025-26, 58% of students in grades 3 to 8 met the state's English standard on the original scale, beating the district's goal a year early, while math proficiency reached 44% against a 55% goal ([D65 Achievement and Accountability Report 2025-26](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1790889798/district65net/c7yzt05rxm3x7jcr3lod/ENGLISH-SY26AchievementandAccountabilityReport.pdf), pp. 23-26).

The district's 2025-26 Achievement and Accountability Report gives a current view of both ends of the K-8 span. The share of students at or above benchmark in early literacy (kindergarten to 2nd grade) already looks much like the share proficient in English by grades 3 to 8:

| Student group | K-2 at or above early literacy benchmark | Grades 3-8 proficient or above in English (state test) |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| White | 74% | 85% |
| Multiracial | 65% | 81% |
| Asian | 63% | 70% |
| Hispanic or Latino | 26% | 53% |
| Black | 25% | 41% |
| Low-income | 26% | 42% |
| Students with IEPs | 24% | 26% |
| English learners | 20% | 27% |

Source: [D65 Achievement and Accountability Report 2025-26](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1790889798/district65net/c7yzt05rxm3x7jcr3lod/ENGLISH-SY26AchievementandAccountabilityReport.pdf), pp. 39-40, 43\. These are different tests with different cut points (the district set its early literacy benchmark at the 67th national percentile, p. 59), so compare the patterns rather than the exact numbers.

The report also found that growth is now close to equal across groups: between 52% and 68% of students in every racial group met their math growth goal, as did 56% of students with IEPs, 55% of English learners, and 54% of low-income students (p. 26). That matches what Reardon described: once students are in school, most groups grow at similar rates, so the gaps that exist in the early grades carry forward. The district's own summary puts it plainly: "The current pace of growth alone will not close these gaps" (p. 26).

Why 3rd grade matters beyond D65: a national study of nearly 4,000 students found that children not reading proficiently by the end of 3rd grade were four times as likely to leave high school without a diploma. For children who had also lived in poverty, the rate rose to 26% ([Annie E. Casey Foundation, 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED518818)).

**What this means for the budget:** the supports that reach children before and during the early grades are where a dollar has the most room to change a trajectory, and cuts there are hardest for later grades to make up.

*A note on dates:* Reardon's original analysis covers 2009 to 2013 (updated through 2024 in the Education Scorecard table above), and the kindergarten readiness and K-3 trajectory figures above are from 2015 to 2019\. The current figures in this section come from the district's 2025-26 report.

## **What research says about cutting school budgets**

### **Cuts show up in student outcomes**

During the Great Recession, per-student spending in U.S. public schools fell about 7%. Northwestern economist Kirabo Jackson and colleagues used differences in how exposed each state's school funding was to the downturn to isolate the effect of those cuts ([Jackson, Wigger, and Xiong, 2021](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/papers.cfm?abstract_id=3102021)). They found:

* A \$1,000 cut in per-student spending lowered test scores by about 1.6 percentile points and reduced the number of college freshmen by about 2.6% ([Education Next](https://www.educationnext.org/test-scores-and-college-going-declined-after-great-recession-spending-cuts/)).  
* Test score effects were larger for children in poorer neighborhoods, and the cuts widened gaps by income and race ([openICPSR project summary](https://www.openicpsr.org/openicpsr/project/119684/version/V1/view)).  
* Districts tried to protect classrooms by cutting construction first, yet the effects still reached students ([Chalkbeat](https://www.chalkbeat.org/2018/1/12/21104108/less-money-for-schools-after-the-recession-meant-lower-test-scores-and-graduation-rates-study-finds/)).

### **How a district spends matters as much as how much**

A second line of research carries a lesson that cuts both ways. University of Illinois at Chicago researchers Paul Zavitkovsky and Steven Tozer studied 709 Illinois districts from 2001 to 2016 ([Evanston RoundTable, July 12, 2017](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2017/07/12/new-report-finds-race-and-income-are-still-strong-predictors-of-achievement-but-school-effectiveness-makes-a-difference/)):

* **Districts with the same poverty level got very different results.** Among districts with about 40% low-income students, the share scoring above the state median ranged from about 20% to about 75%.  
* **School effectiveness could add 0.75 to 2.0 grade levels** of achievement in most districts, by the authors' estimate.  
* **Suburban Chicago districts held steady** even as their low-income enrollment rose by an average of 22 percentage points, while many downstate districts declined.  
* **Leadership quality topped the list of factors.** Strong leadership plus strength in any two other areas (a safe and orderly climate, ambitious instruction, teacher collaboration, and partnership with families) increased the odds tenfold that a school would beat demographic predictions, according to Zavitkovsky.

The authors also found that achievement tracked how equitably regions funded instruction, while cautioning that the pattern does not prove cause and effect.

### **What education researchers prioritize**

In September 2026, The New York Times asked 37 education researchers, including Sean Reardon, to rate 30 ideas for improving student achievement on a scale of 1 to 7 for effectiveness and cost, assuming each was done well ([New York Times, Sept. 28, 2026](https://www.nytimes.com/2026/09/28/upshot/ideas-fixing-schools-ranked.html)). The Times describes the survey as a useful read of expert opinion, not a scientific poll, and it measured only academic achievement. Several results bear on D65's choices:

| Idea | Effectiveness (1-7) | Cost (1-7) | Rank for effectiveness |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Small-group tutoring during the school day | 6.0 | 5.2 | 1st |
| Science of reading (systematic, phonics-based reading instruction) | 5.3 | 3.2 | 2nd |
| Universal pre-K | 4.8 | 5.9 | Tied for 3rd |
| Increase per-student funding | 4.8 | 6.5 | Tied for 3rd |
| Higher pay for teachers at high-poverty schools | 4.8 | 4.1 | Tied for 3rd |
| High-quality, district-vetted curriculum | 4.5 | 3.0 | 9th |
| Non-academic supports for students | 4.5 | 5.6 | 10th |
| Smaller class sizes | 3.8 | 6.5 | 21st |
| Increase mental health spending | 3.5 | 5.0 | 23rd |

Two lessons line up with the rest of this sheet. Early learning and early reading supports rank near the top, consistent with D65's own data showing gaps form before 3rd grade. And the panel stressed execution: the Times reports that the experts saw quality pre-K as highly cost-effective and low-quality pre-K as little more than subsidized child care. The researchers' shared view, in Georgetown's Marguerite Roza's words: "Implementation is everything."

**What this means for D65:** budget reductions carry risk for students, and that risk can be managed. A district that cuts what has weak evidence and protects what works, especially strong school leadership and early-grade supports, can lose dollars without losing as much ground.

## **What specific supports do**

Research on individual programs is uneven. Some supports have strong causal evidence; others are studied mostly through correlations. This table sticks to studies we could verify and names their limits.

| Support | What studies found | Limits | Students who rely on it most |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **Early childhood education** | Long-run gains in graduation, college, and earnings; fewer special education placements | Quality decides results | Children from families with lower incomes; children with developmental delays. See the [early childhood sheet](https://claude.ai/code/artifact/f14e45ec-33bd-41a7-807c-f8a23b12d445) |
| **Elementary school counselors** | One added counselor reduced misbehavior and raised boys' achievement by more than one percentile point ([Carrell and Hoekstra, 2014](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0165176514002766)). In North Carolina elementary and middle schools, more counselors meant higher math scores and fewer absences ([Professional School Counseling, 2022](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ1360060)) | Effects are modest in size; social workers specifically are less studied | Students facing stress at home or behavior challenges |
| **Lower counselor caseloads** | Fewer repeat discipline problems, with larger effects for minority and low-income students ([Carrell and Carrell, 2006](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bpj/bejeap/vcontributions.5y2006i1n11.html)). In high-poverty Missouri schools, meeting the recommended ratio of one counselor per 250 students was associated with better attendance and graduation and fewer discipline incidents ([Lapan et al., 2012](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ996032)). A nationally representative study of elementary schools linked more counselor access to better student behavior and concluded there may be substantial benefits from adding elementary counselors ([Reback, 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20964104/)) | Carrell and Carrell covers one Florida district; the Missouri study is of high schools and shows association, not cause; Reback is national but cannot fully rule out other differences between schools | Students at risk of suspension |
| **School bus eligibility** | Raised attendance and lowered chronic absence for economically disadvantaged students in Michigan ([Education Finance and Policy](https://direct.mit.edu/edfp/article/19/1/1/112222/Another-One-Rides-the-Bus-The-Impact-of-School)). Kindergartners who rode the bus were about 2 percentage points less likely to be chronically absent ([Education Week](https://www.edweek.org/leadership/student-absenteeism-three-studies-to-know/2017/05)) | Little effect for higher-income students, and no detectable effect on test scores in the Michigan study | Families without a car or with inflexible work hours |
| **Early reading support** | Children not reading proficiently by 3rd grade were four times as likely not to finish high school ([Annie E. Casey Foundation, 2011](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=ED518818)) | Shows risk, not which interventions work best | Early readers who arrive behind |

The pattern across these studies: supports that look peripheral on a budget sheet often work through attendance, behavior, and readiness, which are the conditions that let classroom teaching take hold. Their benefits also concentrate among students with the fewest outside resources.

## **Who carries the risk**

When a school support goes away, some families can replace it. They hire a tutor, pay for private preschool or therapy, or drive their child to school. Others cannot. Reardon reported that families in the top income quartile nationally spend about \$9,000 a year on each child, compared with about \$1,300 in the bottom quartile ([Evanston RoundTable, May 17, 2017](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2017/05/17/study-finds-achievement-opportunity-gaps-are-due-primarily-to-differences-that-occur-before-third-grade/)). That difference is the mechanism: cuts to optional supports fall hardest on students whose families have the least room to fill the gap.

In D65, that describes a broad and varied group of families:

The district's own snapshot of its 5,893 students shows how many children are in this position: 37.9% are from low-income families, 20.6% have IEPs, 15.3% are English learners, and 6% changed schools during the year ([D65 board presentation, Sept. 14, 2026](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/FileViewerOrPublic/1247?file=14421838), slide 5). These groups overlap, and together they are a large share of the district.

* **Families with lower incomes.** In 2025-26, 40% of D65 students qualified for free or reduced-price lunch ([D65 Achievement and Accountability Report 2025-26](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1790889798/district65net/c7yzt05rxm3x7jcr3lod/ENGLISH-SY26AchievementandAccountabilityReport.pdf), p. 8). Among low-income students in grades 3 to 8, 42% were proficient in English and 23% in math on the state test, compared with 67% and 54% districtwide (pp. 39-40). The most recent breakdown of family income by race we found is from 2018-19, when 72% of Black students, 63% of Latino students, and 7% of white students came from low-income households ([Evanston RoundTable, Jan. 20, 2020](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2020/01/20/school-district-65-2019-achievement-report-big-drop-in-students-who-are-kindergarten-ready-no-gains-in-college-readiness/)). Families in every group feel the effect of cuts, and these figures show where the effects concentrate.  
* **Students with disabilities.** The share of D65 students with IEPs rose from 17.3% in 2023-24 to 19% in 2025-26 ([D65 Achievement and Accountability Report 2025-26](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1790889798/district65net/c7yzt05rxm3x7jcr3lod/ENGLISH-SY26AchievementandAccountabilityReport.pdf), p. 8). In 2025-26, 26% of students with IEPs were proficient in English and 19% in math on the state test (p. 40), and they accounted for 52% of out-of-school suspension incidents (p. 22). Their required services continue regardless of the budget, and optional supports such as social workers, paraprofessionals, and small-group help can determine how well those services work in a regular classroom.  
* **Students already at risk of losing class time.** Black students make up 23% of D65 enrollment and 71% of out-of-school suspensions ([Evanston Now, Sept. 29, 2026](https://evanstonnow.com/racial-gap-stuns-district-65-leaders/)). Counselor research shows lower caseloads reduce repeat discipline problems, especially for students in this situation.  
* **Families without reliable transportation or stable housing.** In the Michigan bus study above, the attendance benefits of busing were concentrated among economically disadvantaged students.  
* **Multilingual families.** Bilingual teachers and staff are often a family's main point of contact with school. Cuts to those positions should be weighed against how families will stay informed and involved.

Two trends in the district's newest report bear directly on the supports most likely to be cut:

* **Behavior incidents rose sharply.** Total incidents climbed to 10,024 from 5,488 the year before, and the most serious (Level 3 to 5\) rose from 1,283 to 1,982. Black students accounted for 71% of out-of-school suspension incidents, up from a 65% baseline, and students with IEPs for 52%, up from 49%; the low-income share improved to 73% from 77% ([D65 Achievement and Accountability Report 2025-26](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1790889798/district65net/c7yzt05rxm3x7jcr3lod/ENGLISH-SY26AchievementandAccountabilityReport.pdf), pp. 25, 31-33). The suspension percentages come from a small number of cases (48 out-of-school suspension incidents involving 37 students, p. 32), so they can swing from year to year. The direction still matters: this is the moment counselors, social workers, and behavior support staff are most needed.  
* **Student engagement is falling in the early grades.** Only 45% of 3rd and 4th graders responded favorably on engagement in spring 2026, down from 62% in spring 2023 (p. 25). Well-being and belonging held steady.

There is also a bright spot that shows what an optional program can do. In the district's two-way immersion program, 49% of K-2 English learners were at or above benchmark in Spanish early literacy, compared with 20% on the English version (p. 27). The district called this meaningful evidence that biliteracy instruction in the earliest grades is working. Optional components of choice and language programs are on the list of what could be cut.

None of this makes any group a monolith. Plenty of families in each category are doing well. The point is about which families have a cushion when the district pulls back, and which do not.

### **This has happened before**

D65 has faced enrollment drops and budget pressure before, and past responses often moved the same students. When enrollment fell in the 1970s, the district closed schools, including Foster School in 1979, and redrew boundaries. The Evanston Human Relations Commission reported in May 1979 that a Black child was four times as likely as a non-Black child to be bused a distance to school ([Evanston RoundTable, "The Role of Foster School...," March 21, 2019](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2019/03/21/the-role-of-foster-school-in-the-implementation-of-school-district-65s-desegregation-plan-in-1967-and-its-closing-in-1979/); see also ["School District 65: The end of an era," March 8, 2024](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2024/03/08/school-district-65-the-end-of-an-era/)). Thrive65's briefing also cites a 2001 figure that Black students accounted for 57% of non-magnet, non-language-program busing ([Thrive65 Community Briefing, Sept. 2026, slide 9](https://drive.google.com/file/d/1nTz0e6fNV2vHeoG9F_-Z5edXbZ-3-E4o/view)). More recently, the closure of Bessie Rhodes removed what the district itself described as its only fully bilingual school and its most racially and socioeconomically diverse ([D65 Achievement and Accountability Report 2025-26](https://resources.finalsite.net/images/v1790889798/district65net/c7yzt05rxm3x7jcr3lod/ENGLISH-SY26AchievementandAccountabilityReport.pdf), p. 11).

That history is a reason to look closely at where this round of cuts lands, and to ask for an equity impact review of the package before the board votes.

## **Fair points, with context**

**Doing nothing is not an option.** D65's chief financial officer has said the district will run out of cash by fiscal year 2030 without cuts ([Evanston Now, Sept. 2026](https://evanstonnow.com/contemplating-cuts/)). Delay makes the eventual cuts deeper. Protecting students means making smart cuts now.

**D65 is staffed more heavily than its peers, and the gap varies by role.** The district's September presentation compares D65 with seven peer districts ([D65 board presentation, Sept. 14, 2026](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/FileViewerOrPublic/1247?file=14421838), slides 11-13):

| Staff type | D65 students per staff member | Peer average | What it suggests |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Certified staff [1] | 8.5 | 10.6 | About 20% more licensed staff per student than peers |
| Non-certified staff [2] | 10.6 | 23.5 | More than twice as many per student; the highest of the peer group |
| Administrators | 140 (projected 2026\) | 136 statewide | In line with the state after recent reductions |

1. Teachers, specialists, and others who hold a state license  
2. Roles that do not require a license, which can include Paraprofessionals, clerical, and other support staff)

Some staffing reductions are reasonable, and the non-certified comparison stands out. It also covers many of the people who work most directly with students who need extra help. Before cuts are made there, the community should know which roles are counted, how many support IEPs, and which serve students in early grades.

**Costs are rising faster than cuts can keep up.** Compensation is budgeted to grow by \$8.95 million from FY26 to FY27, including a 19.2% rise in benefits ([D65 board presentation, Sept. 14, 2026](https://meetings.boardbook.org/Documents/FileViewerOrPublic/1247?file=14421838), slide 18). Without a change in that trajectory, the district projects running out of cash in FY30 (slide 21).

**Some popular priorities rank poorly on academic value alone.** In the New York Times expert survey described above, smaller class sizes and increased mental health spending both ranked among the least cost-effective ideas for raising test scores. Experts doubted districts can make classes small enough to matter, which research puts at around 15 students. That supports the administration's argument that some staffing ratios can come down, and it complicates the community's stated priority of small classes. On mental health, the survey counted only academic achievement, and the Times notes that students' mental health and well-being matter for other reasons. The research cited in this sheet ties counselors mainly to behavior and attendance, which are the measures that worsened in D65 this year. Those worsening numbers are a reason to ask the district why, before deciding what to cut. Possible factors include structural ones (caseloads after earlier staffing reductions), process ones (how behavior support and referrals work in each building), and policy ones. One policy change worth examining: in 2021 the district phased out self-contained special education classrooms in nine schools and placed more students with disabilities in general education classrooms ([Evanston RoundTable, Oct. 8, 2026](https://evanstonroundtable.com/2026/10/08/analysis-and-viewpoint-d65-class-sizes-an-educational-vision-and-closing-schools-whats-the-priority-and-why/)). Whether that shift, and the loss of separate spaces for small-group and pull-out support, has affected behavior and the demands on support staff is an open question the district should answer with its own data. Class size remains a reasonable place to look for savings if early-grade reading and intervention are protected.

## **Questions to ask the board and administration**

1. For each proposed cut, which students does it serve, and what evidence did the administration weigh about its effect on them?  
2. The administration named supporting the most vulnerable students and keeping mental health and social-emotional staff as community priorities. How does each proposed cut line up with those priorities?  
3. Behavior incidents nearly doubled last year and suspension disproportionality grew. How will potential proposed changes to counselors, social workers, and paraprofessionals affect the district's plan to reverse that?  
4. What does the district's own analysis show about why behavior incidents rose and engagement fell? Has it looked at caseloads, building-level processes, and the effects of the 2021 shift away from self-contained special education classrooms and separate pull-out spaces?  
5. Which roles make up the non-certified staffing comparison, and how many of them support students with IEPs or students in the early grades?  
6. What is the net savings of each cut, considering potential lost grant revenue, reimbursements, and costs that shift elsewhere?  
7. The district's own report credits two-way immersion with strong early biliteracy results. Are any optional parts of language or choice programs proposed for cuts, and what would replace them?  
8. How will the district track the effect of these cuts on attendance, behavior, engagement, early literacy, and 3rd-grade reading, and report back publicly?

## **Talking points**

For a two-minute public comment, choose a couple and add your own experience.

* It's clear the district is going to make significant cuts. I'm asking the board to cut in the places with the least effect on students and to show its reasoning.  
* Non-mandated is a legal label. Counselors, busing, and early childhood all carry it, and research links each of them to better attendance, behavior, or long-term outcomes.  
* Research presented right here in Evanston found that most of our achievement gap is in place by 3rd grade. Early supports are where we can make significant impact.  
* After the Great Recession, school spending cuts lowered test scores and college-going, and the harm was largest in lower-income communities.  
* Illinois districts with the same poverty levels get very different results. Careful choices about what to keep matter as much as the total.  
* Families with means can replace what schools cut. Many D65 families cannot, and they should not carry the heaviest share of this budget.  
* Please protect the supports with the strongest evidence, and publish how each cut was evaluated.
